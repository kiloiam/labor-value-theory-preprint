What the preprint argues

Calling the labor theory of value (LTV) "outdated" tends to conflate two different claims: that a theory has been falsified within its own premises, and that the historical premises the theory describes have themselves disappeared. This preprint argues the second.

It treats the LTV as a historically specific theory grounded in wage labor, socially necessary labor time and the universality of commodity exchange, not as a universal principle valid for every social formation. Its conclusion is therefore not that Marx miscalculated, but that the social form on which the value-category depends has reached its historical endpoint.

That distinction matters for searching. If you are looking for empirical or econometric debates about whether labor values predict prices, you want the transformation-problem literature. If you are asking whether automated production dissolves the historical preconditions of value altogether, that is the question this paper addresses.

This is an argument from an online preprint, not a peer-reviewed result and not a statement of scholarly consensus.

Frequently asked questions

Does this mean Marx was wrong?
No. The paper distinguishes a theory being falsified from the disappearance of the historical conditions the theory describes. It treats the LTV as coherent within its own premises and asks what happens when those premises dissolve.
How does this differ from the usual "LTV is obsolete" claim?
Usual objections concern price prediction or competing factor theories of value. This paper does not enter that econometric debate; it asks whether the social preconditions that make the value-category meaningful still exist.
Has the claim been empirically verified?
No. The paper offers a revised value model incorporating automated technological capital and a theoretical "four-fold impossibility" argument. It is a theoretical criterion, not an empirical finding, and the preprint is not peer reviewed.
