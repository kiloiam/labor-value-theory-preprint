What the preprint argues

In the Marxian framework, the tendency of the rate of profit to fall is usually linked to a rising organic composition of capital: as constant capital grows relative to living labor while surplus value is attributed to living labor, downward pressure on the profit rate emerges, alongside a set of counteracting factors. Both the interpretation and the empirical standing of the law remain contested.

Its connection to the labor theory of value lies in a shared premise: valorization depends on the labor power purchased by variable capital. That is why contemporary debates about automation, productivity and profitability repeatedly return to the question of whether living labor remains the source of value.

This preprint examines the limiting case of that premise: it argues that once automated systems can in principle operate independently of living labor, variable capital is systematically and irreversibly expelled, surplus-value production tends toward exhaustion, and profit is transmuted into a 'negative political cost of social stabilization under private property relations' (P). This is not an econometric test of the falling-rate-of-profit law; it is a non-peer-reviewed theoretical claim.

Frequently asked questions

Does the preprint prove the falling-rate-of-profit law?
No. It offers no econometric test; it asks whether the valorization mechanism itself can still hold once living labor is systematically expelled.
Is 'negative political cost P' a new concept?
It is introduced by this preprint to describe the form profit takes when it no longer results from valorization. Its definition and argument chain require the full text and have not been peer reviewed.
Is 'automated technological capital' the same as the organic composition of capital?
No. The preprint constructs a revised value model incorporating automated technological capital to analyze a limiting tendency; the terms should not be equated.
